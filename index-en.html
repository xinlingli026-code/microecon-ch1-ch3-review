Chapter 1 Introduction to Economics

Subject of study: the scarcity of resources, choice and their use · Micro and macro · Research methods · A brief history of economic thought

1. The subject of study of economics

1
The meaning of “economics” (three levels)
① Money and income; ② thrift, saving, efficiency; ③ the activities of social production, distribution, exchange and consumption, together with the institutions that organise these activities.
Cultural backgroundIn classical Chinese the term was tied to the idea of “governing the state and relieving the people” and of “administering the state and assisting the nation”; in the West “economics” originally referred to household management (the management of the household estate).
2
The evolution of the name and the subject matter of economics: Xenophon's Oeconomicus (Household Management) in ancient Greece → Antoine de Montchrétien, Traité d'économie politique (Political Economy for Kings and Queens), France, 1615 → Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, 1776 → Keynes, The General Theory, 1936 → Samuelson, Economics, 1948.
3
Two major theoretical systems: Marxist political economy studies the laws of development of social economic systems and of production relations; Western economics studies the rational allocation and the full utilization of scarce resources.

I. The subject of study of economics: scarcity — choice — use

  • The scarcity of resources: the finiteness of resources relative to the unlimited range of human wants.
  • Unlimited wants: a Qing-dynasty saying — “Rushing about all day long for nothing but hunger; the moment one is fed, one thinks of clothes…” (often quoted in examinations).
  • Two meanings of scarcity: ① resources are finite relative to wants; ② resources are scarce relative to other resources (they have to be chosen among precisely because they have uses).
  • The choice and allocation of resources: how to use given resources to produce various economic goods so as to satisfy needs better → the allocation problem, which contains three basic questions:
    # What to produce, and how much (What & How many) → the production possibilities frontier
    # How to produce (How) → producer behaviour (production theory, cost–revenue theory)
    # For whom to produce (For whom) → factor price determination and the theory of income distribution
  • The production possibilities curve (PPC): the locus of the maximum combinations of output that society can obtain under given resource and technology constraints. Points inside the curve = inefficient allocation, output that cannot be attained; points on the curve = efficient.
  • The utilization of resources: how to make better use of existing scarce resources in order to produce more economic goods. The reasons why resources are left idle and wasted:
    Economic cycles and economic growth
    Why output cannot always stay on the PPC
    Full employment
    Why resources are not fully utilized
    Inflation
    How changes in the purchasing power of money affect output
Definition of economics (must be memorised)Economics is the discipline that studies the allocation and utilization of scarce resources under the market economic system.
The institutional trichotomy: the planned economic system, the market economic system, the mixed economic system.

The three basic assumptions of micro-economics (a frequent examination point) ① Market clearing (clean markets): under spontaneous price adjustment the goods, labour and capital markets clear and resources are fully utilized.
② Perfect rationality: producers and consumers are both perfectly self-interested economic agents who act on the maximization principle and know how to implement it.
③ Perfect information: information is plentiful, its distribution is perfectly symmetric, and acquiring it costs nothing.
The differences and the connections between micro and macro Differences: five points differ — subject of study, problem solved, basic assumptions, method, core theory.
Connections: ① they complement each other; ② both take the market economic system as their premise; ③ micro-economics is the foundation of macro-economics.

3. The research methods of economics

1. Economic models and the six-step procedure

An economic model: a theoretical structure describing the interrelationships among the relevant economic variables. The typical steps are:

define the variables → make the assumptions → formulate the hypothesis → explain and predict → test the predictions → evaluate and choose (if the test is passed the theory is accepted; if it is not passed it is rejected and a new theory is put forward; the assumptions and hypotheses may be modified)
  • Endogenous variable: a variable that the theory seeks to explain; exogenous variable: a variable determined by factors outside the theory.
  • Stock: the value of a variable existing at a point in time; flow: the value of a variable arising over a period of time.
  • How to distinguish a stock from a flow: ① can the values be added directly (stocks cannot, flows can); ② is it related to time (stocks are not, flows are).
Common pitfallEndogenous/exogenous variables ≠ independent/dependent variables. In Q = a − bP, a and b are constants, and both P and Q are endogenous variables; it is national consumption policy, income, consumption preferences and the like — factors not captured by the model — that are exogenous; within this function P is the independent variable and Q the dependent variable.

2. Positive analysis and normative analysis

Positive analysis
It answers the question “what is the case”, taking the facts as they are and carrying no value judgement.
Normative analysis
It answers the question “what ought to be the case”, reasoning about what should happen on the basis of value judgements.

3. Equilibrium analysis and marginal analysis

  • Equilibrium analysis: given that external factors are known and fixed, it studies the conditions for equilibrium to arise. It is divided into partial equilibrium (a single market, other conditions constant) and general equilibrium (all markets taken as a whole and influencing one another).
  • Marginal analysis: the margin is the increment of an economic variable; a marginal quantity is the increase in another variable caused by a one-unit increase in the given variable (e.g. marginal cost = the increase in total cost caused by a one-unit increase in output).

4. Static, comparative static and dynamic analysis

Chapter 2 Demand, Supply and the Equilibrium Price

Theory of demand · Theory of supply · Theory of the equilibrium price · Theory of elasticity and its applications

1. Theory of demand

1
The meaning of demand: the quantity of a given good that consumers are willing and able to buy at each price level during a given period. Three key points: ① it refers to one particular good; ② it describes an inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded; ③ it is the unity of the desire to purchase and the ability to pay — actual rather than potential demand.
2
The demand curve: a curve showing the inverse relationship between the price of a good and the quantity demanded; it slopes down to the right.
D = f(a, b, c, d, …) general demand function
D = f(P) linear: D = a − bP or D = aP − b (a and b are positive constants)

3. Consumer incomedepends on the goodnormal goods: same; inferior goods: opposite; basic necessities: essentially unchanged

Others—climate, holidays, unexpected events (epidemics → masks, disinfectant alcohol, protective clothing; Valentine's Day → roses, chocolates)
The law of demand and its exceptions The law of demand: ceteris paribus, the quantity demanded of a good and its price move in opposite directions (price rises → quantity falls).
Exception: the Giffen good — a price rise actually increases the quantity demanded (as with some inferior goods).

2. Theory of supply

1
The meaning of supply: the quantity of a given good that producers are willing and able to supply at each price level during a given period; it is the unity of the desire to supply and the capacity to supply.
2
The supply curve: the relationship between price and quantity supplied, which move in the same direction; it slopes up to the right.
S = f(a, b, c, d, …) general supply function
S = f(P) linear: S = −c + dP non-linear: S = λPβ (c, d, λ and β are positive constants)
Factors affecting supplyDirectionExplanation
1. The price of the good itselfsameprice rises → supply increases
2. The price of related goodsdepends on the relationComplements: opposite; substitutes: same; goods produced from the same resources (rice–wheat, defence goods–civilian goods): opposite
3. The state of production technologysametechnological progress raises supply
4. The prices of the factors of productionoppositehigher factor prices raise costs
5. Producers' expectations about the futuredepends on the caseoptimistic expectations → reduced current supply and expansion of production capacity → greater future supply; pessimistic expectations the other way round
6. Government economic (industrial) policydepends on the policyencouragement of investment and production (tax cuts, 5G/new energy/AI/the internet/big data/UHV transmission/intercity high-speed rail) → supply increases; restriction (textiles, steel, cement, chemicals) → supply falls
The law of supply and its exceptions The law of supply: ceteris paribus, the quantity supplied and the price move in the same direction.
Exceptions: the supply of labour (a higher wage may reduce the supply of labour — the McGregor X theory: substitution effect versus income effect), and the supply of land, antiques and works of art (quantity supplied is almost unchanged when the price rises).

3. Theory of the equilibrium price

  • The equilibrium price: the price at which the quantity supplied of a good equals the quantity demanded; the corresponding quantity demanded (supplied) is the equilibrium quantity.
  • How it forms: P > P₀ → excess supply → P falls and Q moves toward equilibrium; P < P₀ → excess demand → P rises and Q moves toward equilibrium. In reality, wages (labour market) and interest rates (capital market) are also market equilibrium prices.
Equilibrium solution: Qd = Qs → solve the system for P₀ and Q₀
Example: Qd = 22 − 5P, Qs = −5 + 4P ⇒ 22 − 5P = −5 + 4P ⇒ P = 3, Qd = Qs = 7

Government intervention in prices: price policy

① A support price (price floor)
A minimum price set by the government above the equilibrium price (e.g. agricultural product prices, minimum wage standards).
Result: excess supply, with a surplus of Qs − Qd.
Remedies: ① government purchase (adding to stocks or to exports); ② subsidies to producers; ③ restriction of the area planted.
Positive stabilizes and supports agricultural development Negative ① excess supply ② increased government budgetary expenditure ③ may trigger international trade wars in agricultural products
② A restricted price (price ceiling)
A maximum price set by the government below the equilibrium price, used in wartime, in disasters and other special periods and for basic necessities.
Result: excess demand. The government must operate a monopoly or introduce rationing (supply by coupons, e.g. grain coupons from 1955 to 1993).
Positive ① guarantees basic living needs ② promotes social equality and stability
Negative ① discourages an increase in supply and restrains demand → excess demand ② easily gives rise to “black market” transactions and corrupts social morals

4. Theory of elasticity

1. Price elasticity of demand Ed

Meaning: the ratio of the percentage change in the quantity demanded of a good to the percentage change in its price, i.e. the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price.

Point elasticity: Ed = (dQ / dP) × (P / Q) Arc elasticity: Ed = −(ΔQ / ΔP) × (P̄ / Q̄)
Note: ① it is a ratio of percentages rather than of absolute quantities; ② quantity demanded and price move in opposite directions, so the elasticity is negative; in practice the absolute value is used; ③ elasticity differs at different points on the same demand curve — points in the upper left are more elastic than points in the lower right; ④ point elasticity is a special case of arc elasticity.
TypeCoefficientMeaningCurve shapeTypical goods
Perfectly elasticEd → ∞demand is unlimited at the given price levelhorizontal line—
ElasticEd > 1the percentage change in quantity demanded exceeds the percentage change in pricerelatively flatternon-necessities, luxuries, recreational services
Unit elasticEd = 1the two ratios are equal——
Inelastic0 < Ed < 1the percentage change in quantity demanded is less than the percentage change in pricerelatively steeperdaily necessities such as rice, salt, vegetables and fuel
Perfectly inelasticEd = 0quantity demanded does not change however much the price changesvertical line—
The 5 factors that determine the price elasticity of demand ① The degree of necessity of the good: the more necessary it is, the more inelastic (luxuries are highly elastic).
② The degree of substitutability of the good: the more close substitutes it has, the more elastic it is.
③ The share of the good in total household expenditure: directly proportional to elasticity.
④ How long the good is used: durable consumer goods (long life) are elastic; non-durables are inelastic.
⑤ The breadth of the uses of the good: directly proportional to elasticity (the more uses it has, the more a price rise cuts back its less important uses).

EI = % change in demand ÷ % change in income

Classifying goods by income elasticity (must be memorised) EI > 0 → normal good; EI < 0 → inferior good; among normal goods EI > 1 → luxury, 0 < EI < 1 → necessity.

3. Cross elasticity of demand Exy

  • Meaning: the ratio of the percentage change in the demand for one of two related goods to the percentage change in the price of the other.
  • Complementary relation (they move in opposite directions) → the cross elasticity is negative; substitute relation (they move in the same direction) → the cross elasticity is positive; unrelated goods → zero.
  • The larger the absolute value, the closer the complementarity / the stronger the substitution; conversely, the sign of the coefficient can be used to infer the relationship between the goods.

4. Price elasticity of supply ES

ES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price (the computation of point and arc elasticity is exactly the same as for demand elasticity)

Perfectly elasticES → ∞supply is unlimited at the given price; horizontal line

The 5 factors that determine the price elasticity of supply ① The length of the period (the most important): in the short run only variable factors (labour, raw materials) can be adjusted and plant and equipment cannot → inelastic; in the long run all factors are adjustable → elastic.
② The ease of production and the length of the production cycle: easy to produce and short cycles → elastic.
③ The type of production technology: capital-intensive technology cannot easily be scaled → inelastic; labour-intensive → elastic.
④ The supply elasticity of the factors of production: the more elastic the factors, the more elastic the product.
⑤ The size of current output: when output is very small it is easy to expand → elastic; as it approaches its limit, elasticity tends to 0.
The short run and the long run in micro-economics Short run: a period in which not all factors of production can be adjusted in order to reach a given level of output; long run: a period in which all factors of production can be adjusted.

2. The cobweb theorem

  • Origin: proposed in the 1930s by Schultz (USA) and Ricchi (Italy); named in 1934 by Kaldor, because the diagram resembles a spider's web. Core proposition: once supply–demand equilibrium has been disturbed, market forces need not restore equilibrium automatically.
  • Basic assumptions: ① current output determines the current price Pt = f(QtS); ② the current price determines next period's output QSt+1 = f(Pt).

3. The sharing of a government tax

  • Direct tax: levied on property and income; the taxpayer is the bearer of the tax and it cannot be shifted.
  • Indirect tax: levied on goods and services; the taxpayer is not entirely the bearer of the tax and it can be shifted; divided into specific (per-unit) taxes and ad valorem taxes.
  • The effect of a specific tax: the supply curve shifts vertically upward by T, the demand curve is unchanged → the equilibrium price rises and the equilibrium quantity falls.

Ed < ESthe tax is borne mainly by consumers; the more elastic supply is, the more consumers bear; with perfectly elastic supply consumers bear all of it
Ed = ESproducers and consumers share the burden equally
Consumer burden = price paid after tax − price paid before tax = P₁ − P₀
Producer burden = sales revenue before tax − sales revenue after tax = P₀ − (P₁ − T) = P₀ − P₂
Example: Qd = 120 − 4P, Qs = −20 + 3P ⇒ 120 − 4P = −20 + 3P ⇒ P₀ = 20, Q₀ = 40
a tax of 7 per unit ⇒ new supply Qs = −20 + 3(P − 7) = −41 + 3P ⇒ 120 − 4P = −41 + 3P ⇒ P = 23, Q = 28

Chapter 3 Theory of Consumer Behaviour

Utility theory · Cardinal utility theory (marginal utility analysis) · Ordinal utility theory (indifference curve analysis) · Substitution effect and income effect · Price consumption curve and income consumption curve

1. Utility theory

1
The meaning of utility: the degree of satisfaction a consumer obtains from consuming a given good.
Utility versus use value: use value is objective (the physical properties of a good that satisfy a want); utility is subjective and varies with person, with time and with place.
2
Cardinal utility theory (Jevons, Walras): utility is measurable and additive, so it can be expressed in cardinal numbers → method of analysis: the marginal utility analysis.
3
Ordinal utility theory (Pareto, Hicks): utility as a psychological feeling cannot be measured and cannot be added up; only its relative ordering can be expressed, in ordinal numbers → method of analysis: the indifference curve analysis.
4
The challenge that utility theory poses to traditional theory: ① it reinterprets the meaning of exchange; ② it questions the determination of price by value (a product that is not a labour product still has a price; a high value may go with a low price; the problem of decomposing labour; prices need not fluctuate around a value axis); ③ the way to make money is to create utility; ④ marginal utility determines price.
Conclusion: price is determined jointly by labour value and utility — utility sets the upper bound of price and the producer's cost sets the lower bound.

2. Cardinal utility theory — the marginal utility analysis

Total utility TU = f(X) or f(X, Y); marginal utility MU = the increase in total utility from consuming one additional unit of the good
TU = ΣMU; MU > 0 → TU increases; MU < 0 → TU decreases; MU = 0 → TU is at a maximum
  • Economic good: consuming more raises total utility (MU > 0); economic bad: consuming more lowers total utility (MU < 0); economically neutral good: MU = 0.

The law of diminishing marginal utility

Meaning: within a given period, holding the consumption of other goods constant, as the consumption of a given good increases, the marginal utility obtained from each successive additional unit consumed always diminishes (even the finest dish loses its appeal when one has eaten too much of it).

Causes: ① physiological or psychological reasons (the stimulating effect becomes weaker); ② the diversity of uses of a good (uses are always ranked from the most important to the less important and then again to the more important). Exceptions: goods that work as a matched set, and goods for which one has an irresistible craving.

Consumer equilibrium

  • Meaning: given income and the prices of goods, the state in which the consumer attains the maximum utility from consuming a given quantity of goods.
  • Three assumptions: ① the tastes of the consumer are given; ② income is given, and the marginal utility of each unit of money is the same; ③ the prices of goods are given.
  • Condition: the consumer spends all of his income on buying various goods, and the ratio of marginal utility to price is the same for every good.
MUX / PX = MUY / PY = λ (λ = the marginal utility of a unit of money)
Proof (Lagrange / marginal substitution): take the partial derivatives of a multivariable function, treating the other variables as constants when differentiating with respect to one variable
Example: I = 100, PX = 10, PY = 20 → equilibrium solution (X = 4, Y = 3), total utility 29 is at a maximum

The law of diminishing marginal utility and the law of demand

The price a consumer is willing to pay depends on marginal utility: the larger the MU, the higher the price he is willing to pay. If MU diminishes while the marginal utility of money λ stays constant ⇒ a fall in price ⇒ quantity demanded and price move in opposite directions.

MU = λ × P ⇒ P = MU / λ (MU diminishing and λ constant ⇒ P falls)

Consumer surplus and producer surplus

  • Consumer surplus (introduced by Marshall): the difference between the total amount the consumer is willing to pay for a given quantity of a good and the total amount actually paid. Graphically: the area between the demand curve and the price line; as consumption increases, the consumer surplus of each additional unit diminishes.
  • Producer surplus: the difference between the total amount producers are willing to accept and the total amount actually received at the market price (the area between the supply curve and the price line).
  • Social welfare = consumer surplus + producer surplus (= total surplus).

3. Ordinal utility theory — the indifference curve analysis

1
The indifference curve (an iso-utility curve): a curve showing the combinations of different quantities of two goods that give the consumer exactly the same utility.
2
Three basic assumptions: ① completeness of preferences; ② transitivity of preferences (A>B, B>C ⇒ A>C); ③ non-satiation of preferences (“more is better” — all goods are economic goods).
3
Four characteristics: ① it slopes down to the right and its slope is negative; ② there are infinitely many in a given plane, and the farther from the origin, the higher the utility; ③ no two of them can intersect (otherwise “more is better” would be contradicted); ④ in general they are convex to the origin (as determined by the diminishing marginal rate of substitution).

The marginal rate of substitution MRS

  • Meaning: the amount of Y the consumer must give up in order to gain one more unit of X while keeping total utility constant; it is exactly the slope of the indifference curve at that point (found by drawing the tangent).
  • The law of diminishing marginal rate of substitution: holding total utility constant, as consumption of X keeps increasing, each additional unit of X replaces less and less Y (note: what diminishes is the absolute value).
  • Cause: diminishing marginal utility — the MU of X becomes smaller while the MU of Y becomes larger.
  • Relation to the shape of the curve: a diminishing MRS ⇒ the curve is steeper in its upper-left segment and flatter in its lower-right segment ⇒ convex to the origin. A straight line (perfect substitutes) does not satisfy diminishing MRS; a curve concave to the origin does not satisfy it either.
  • Special cases: perfect substitutes (e.g. banknotes and coins) → a straight line; perfect complements (e.g. lenses and frames) → right angles.

Consumer equilibrium under the ordinal approach

  • Conclusion: the combination of goods corresponding to the point of tangency between the budget constraint line and an indifference curve is the consumer equilibrium point.
  • Reason: indifference curves outside the budget line have higher utility but are unattainable; those inside it are attainable but do not maximize utility; hence equilibrium must lie on the budget line, and only the tangency point maximizes utility along that line.
  • Two conditions: ① the equilibrium point lies on the budget constraint line: I = X·PX + Y·PY; ② the two lines have equal slopes at the tangency point: slope of the budget line = slope of the indifference curve (MRS) ⇒ PX/PY = MRSXY = MUX/MUY.
  • Comparison with the cardinal approach: MUX/PX = MUY/PY and MRSXY = PX/PY are equivalent; the equilibrium conditions are the same, only the method of analysis differs.

4. Substitution effect, income effect, price effect · consumption curves

1. Hicks's substitution effect, income effect and price effect (when the price falls)

  • Substitution effect: when the prices of other goods are unchanged, the good whose price has fallen is relatively cheaper, and consumers substitute it for the more expensive good and increase their demand for it → the substitution effect > 0 (opposite to the price). In the analysis, real income is assumed to be constant (utility constant).
  • Income effect: with nominal income unchanged, a fall in the price raises real income, thereby increasing demand for the good. The analysis considers only the change in real income.
  • Price effect = substitution effect + income effect, i.e. the increase in quantity demanded caused by the fall in price.

A reminder of a common pitfallWhen the price of a Giffen good falls, quantity demanded decreases, because the absolute value of the negative income effect exceeds the substitution effect — this is the only exception to the law of demand.

2. The price consumption curve (PCC) and the demand curve

  • The price consumption curve: the locus of the tangency points between a number of budget constraint lines and a number of indifference curves, traced out as the price of a good changes while income and the prices of other goods remain constant; every point on the curve is a consumer equilibrium.
  • Deriving the demand curve from the PCC: as the price of X changes, the equilibrium quantity moves in the opposite direction to price ⇒ the demand curve slopes down to the right; every point on the demand curve shows the quantity demanded that yields the maximum utility at the corresponding price.

3. The income consumption curve (ICC) and the Engel curve

  • The income consumption curve: the locus of the tangency points between a number of budget constraint lines and a number of indifference curves, traced out as income changes while the prices of goods remain constant; every point on the curve is a consumer equilibrium.
  • The Engel curve: a curve showing the relationship between the consumer's income and the quantity demanded of a given good. For a normal good it slopes up to the right; for an inferior good it slopes up to the left.
  • Engel's law: as income keeps rising, the share of food expenditure in household expenditure keeps falling, while the share of other expenditure keeps rising. The lower the Engel coefficient, the higher the standard of living.
The Engel coefficient and the standard of living (standard empirical benchmarks)above 59%: poverty; 50%–59%: subsistence; 40%–50%: comfortably off; 30%–40%: affluent; below 30%: the most affluent. In 2019 the figure for China was 28.2%.
